# pps_tax_and_funding_report

_See [PPS_Tax_and_Funding_Report_R1.pdf](releases/PPS_Tax_and_Funding_Report_R1.pdf) for the full report._

Data from the Sabin Elementary School catchment area and Portland Public Schools (PPS) district indicate that property tax revenue has increased steadily from 2021 through 2025, outpacing both district cost increases and inflation indexes.

Despite this, PPS is planning to close Sabin and other district schools after the 2026-27 school year, claiming to need fewer facilities to offer the same level of instruction for students.  This raises the question of why slightly increased funding would lead to a significant decrease in service.

The conclusion of the report is summarized here.

# Conclusion

Portland Public Schools (PPS) claims to be facing a $ 65M shortfall in the 2027-28 school year.  The resulting drop in school resources is claimed to be the reason for consolidating schools into fewer buildings while leaving abandoned schools in the middles of neighborhoods throughout the city.

After the 2023-24 school year, the federal ESSER Covid relief money ran out.  PPS spent the General Fund down approximately $ 60M from 2023-24 to 2026-27, when it only had $ 41M remaining.  Hence the need for budget cuts under the current funding model.

The funding formulas in Oregon statutes ORS 327.008, 327.011 and 327.013 result in taxyers in the PPS district effectively redistributing $ 123M of their tax payments to other school districts in the state.  Note this this amount is almost double the claimed 2027-28 shortfall.

Thus taxyers in the PPS district already do pay enough taxes to keep the existing level of service, but part of these taxes is taken by the state and given to other districts.

## Further Investigations

If the taxpayers into Portland Public Schools are paying money out to districts in the rest of the state, it would be interesting to know other districts' permanent and local option tax revenue, their share of ADMw, and whether their "equitable grant" in section 3.2.3 results in a net outflow from that particular district.  From there, it would be interesting the impact or non-impact of Local Option taxes through the state.  Finally, it would be useful to explore voter support for Local Options in districts across the state.

Anecdotally, Portland Public Schools serves a disproportionate number of students with special needs compared to the state as a whole; this is a reason frequently given by those who think the funding formula ought to change the "weighting" of these students in the grant formula.  Portland Public Schools also serves the state's largest longstanding black community.  There is a compelling argument that keeping funding inside the PPS district is necessary to equitably serve these Oregon residents.
